AAI-590 Capstone Project Guidelines
Your capstone project requires you to research, propose, implement, test, evaluate, and report on an applied machine learning problem of your choice. This will be an extensive project that makes up 91% of the course points between the weekly submissions and the final deliverables. You have broad latitude to pick a problem of interest, and the project should end up as a valuable portfolio item for all students. Read through the following documentation closely to ensure that your project meets all requirements. There will be three main deliverables for this project, with an additional peer evaluation for group projects:

Final Project Report
Final Project Code Base (GitHub Repository)
Final Project Presentation
Team Peer Evaluation (For group projects only)
Teams
Students are encouraged to work in groups of 3-4, as we have found that this allows students to complete more complex projects within the limited 7-week course. However, individual projects and groups of 2 are also permitted and will have slightly different expectations in terms of the project scope and final deliverables. See the following sections for more details.

Teams for the project must be formed within the first week of the course, as the first project deliverable will be due at the end of Module 1. Students will choose their own teams on Canvas, but are encouraged to communicate with classmates on Slack to form teams with similar interests. Contact your instructor if you would like any assistance in finding or forming a team.

Even students completing individual projects must join a team (with only one team member) on Canvas for grading purposes.

Scope of the Project
You and your team must scope this project in advance such that it can be completed with each team member contributing 15 hours per week for 7 weeks. It is important that you do not over-scope the project, or it will not be completed within the time available in the course, but it is also important not to choose a project that is too simple to showcase the skills that you have gained during your time in the program. If you have questions about project scope, reach out to your instructor who can help guide you to an appropriately sized project.

Your professor will provide details on how best to schedule with them.

Topic for the Project
The project may be any applied AI problem that the team is interested in and chooses to build.
Students will be required to identify and cleanse a data set, choose a tool, and an algorithm.
Module 1 includes several lists of project ideas that you can use to help brainstorm and find datasets. However, you are not obligated to use a project from these lists, and some of the projects listed are not appropriate for the scope of this project.
Your project must demonstrate original work, so closely following an existing project tutorial is not acceptable.
Your project must be sufficiently complex and/or challenging to qualify as a capstone project–this should not be something that you would have completed as a final project in another course. Common routes to achieve appropriate complexity include:
an interesting data ingestion/processing pipeline, often involving a machine learning element
a novel or complex model architecture (something like an ensemble model, or implementing an experimental architecture)
a comparison of multiple models on your chosen task
development of a complete machine learning system for deployment in a platform such as AWS
Machine Learning Method Selection
The project can include traditional Machine Learning methods (e.g., Linear Regression, Decision Trees, etc.), but it must include at least one neural network/deep learning based model (e.g., Feed Forward Network/MLP, RNN, CNN, Transformer, GAN, etc.).
Your project must include some element of model training. This can be satisfied by building a model from scratch, using a library like PyTorch, Keras, and/or Tensorflow, or by fine-tuning a pre-trained model using a library like HuggingFace.
You may not simply follow along with a project tutorial–you can use a tutorial as a motivation or resource for your project, but you and your team must produce something that is original work.
Training different types of models can vary greatly in the required time, data, and compute resources. Consider these requirements when choosing your methods and plan accordingly.

1 Introduction
The modern food and dietary supplement industry frequently obscures the true nutritional quality of products through complex chemical formulations and aggressive marketing. Standard nutritional tracking applications often focus primarily on macronutrients and caloric intake, creating a significant analytical blind spot for health-washing, where highly manufactured items are marketed as healthy simply because they fit a particular macronutrient profile. For example, consumers attempting to evaluate the quality of products such as commercial protein powders may encounter highly synthesized ingredient labels that are difficult to interpret without specialized knowledge. Because caloric values alone do not fully represent the metabolic impact of food, and the increasing consumption of ultra-processed foods has been linked to metabolic and cardiovascular disease, there is a growing need for automated systems capable of evaluating the processing level and chemical composition of foods beyond simple calorie counting (Monteiro et al., 2019; Srour et al., 2019).
The primary end users of this Artificial Intelligence product are health-conscious consumers, clinical nutritionists, and individuals managing strict dietary constraints who require rapid, context-aware assessments of food quality. These users need to look beyond the basic nutrition facts panel to understand the structural integrity of the food they consume and whether it aligns with their highly specific personal profiles, such as managing medical conditions, adhering to halal restrictions, or hitting specific micronutrient goals. This system eliminates the need to conduct exhaustive manual research on individual chemical additives while navigating the grocery store, allowing users to make informed, split-second dietary decisions that are personalized to their unique health constraints. This is useful because it reduces label interpretation time from minutes to seconds and helps users avoid misleading “macro-friendly” products that may still be metabolically disruptive due to additives and industrial processing.
To train the underlying machine learning models, this project will utilize tabular data sourced from the Open Food Facts database. This dataset is a large, open-source repository containing hundreds of thousands of consumer food products, including detailed macronutrient breakdowns per 100 grams, additive indicators, and NOVA classification labels (Open Food Facts, 2024). Key input fields used in this study include per-100g nutrition values such as sugars, fiber, protein, saturated fat, and sodium, along with ingredient text, additive counts, and additive categories such as sweeteners, emulsifiers, and preservatives. These variables allow the model to capture both the nutritional composition and the additive complexity associated with different levels of industrial food processing.
However, relying solely on a static database in a production environment introduces a significant risk of data drift due to the food industry's frequent, unannounced recipe reformulations. Therefore, if this project were deployed as a live commercial AI product, the real-time data would bypass historical barcode lookups. Instead, the live system would utilize an Optical Character Recognition (OCR) pipeline via a user's mobile device camera to extract the exact, real-time tabular nutritional data directly from the physical packaging label, ensuring the models always evaluate the most accurate chemical matrix.
The ultimate goal of this capstone project is to develop a hybrid machine learning pipeline and rule-based recommendation engine that shifts dietary analysis from static macronutrient counting to a more comprehensive evaluation of food processing characteristics. The final product will be deployed as an interactive application that translates complex ingredient labels into a simple, personalized dashboard. The proposed system architecture will utilize a supervised baseline model, specifically XGBoost, to classify a product into a standardized processing tier based on the NOVA classification system. In parallel, an unsupervised deep learning model, implemented as an Autoencoder neural network trained on the nutritional profiles of minimally processed foods, will generate a reconstruction error score for each input sample. This reconstruction error will serve as an anomaly score indicating how much the nutritional profile of a product deviates from typical whole-food patterns.
2 Data Summary
The Open Food Facts dataset serves as the primary data source for this project and contains nutritional, ingredient, and processing information for a large number of packaged food products (Open Food Facts, 2024). Each entry includes detailed nutritional values per 100 grams, ingredient-related information, additive indicators, and the NOVA classification label, which categorizes foods into four groups based on their level of industrial processing. The NOVA group variable (1–4), which represents increasing levels of industrial food processing, is used as the primary target variable for the classification task. Predictor variables include key nutritional features such as energy, fat, carbohydrates, sugars, fiber, protein, and sodium, along with additive-related indicators that may signal higher levels of food processing.
Because the dataset is crowdsourced, it contains missing values and potential inconsistencies that must be addressed prior to model training. Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) was performed to evaluate dataset structure, identify missing values, and examine the distribution of key nutritional variables. Rows missing the target variable (nova_group) were removed to ensure valid supervised training. Continuous nutritional variables were stabilized by capping extreme values at the 1st and 99th percentiles to reduce the influence of outliers. Remaining missing numerical values were handled using median imputation, while missing additive counts were filled with zero where appropriate.
Statistical analysis was conducted to evaluate whether selected nutritional and additive features differ across the NOVA processing categories. Non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to assess differences in feature distributions between processing tiers. Results indicated statistically significant variation in several nutritional and additive features across the NOVA groups, supporting their suitability as predictive signals for the classification models.
Correlation analysis was also performed to examine relationships among nutritional variables. As expected, several features exhibited strong correlations, including the deterministic relationship between sodium and salt measurements, where salt is derived from sodium through standard nutritional labeling conversions. Tree-based models used in this project are generally robust to multicollinearity; however, redundant features were removed and numerical variables were standardized when preparing data for distance-based methods used in downstream analysis. These preprocessing and validation steps ensure that the dataset is suitable for training machine learning models capable of identifying patterns associated with different levels of industrial food processing.
